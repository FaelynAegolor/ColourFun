Grid & proportion

Two ways to get proportions right before you commit to tone and detail: the grid method, which transfers a reference square by square, and comparative measuring, which checks every length and angle against one unit taken from the subject itself.

The methods

The grid is a transfer technique for working from a flat reference. Measuring is an observational discipline that works from life or from a reference. Both replace guessing with comparison.

  • Grid methodRule the same number of square cells over the reference and the paper, then copy one cell at a time. Because every cell scales by the same factor, the whole image enlarges without distortion.
  • Diagonals and labelsDiagonals split each cell into triangles, giving extra reference lines where contours turn sharply. Lettered columns and numbered rows let you find your place again at once.
  • Sight-sizeSet the drawing beside the subject and stand where both appear the same size. Measurements then transfer at 1 : 1, and you compare drawing and subject from the same spot.
  • Comparative measuringChoose one unit from the subject, such as a head height, and express every other length as a multiple of it. Proportion is about ratios, so the unit matters more than any absolute size.
  • Plumb and level linesA truly vertical (plumb) or horizontal (level) sight line shows what sits directly above, below or beside what: the pit of the neck over the standing ankle, or which shoulder is higher.
  • AnglesLay the pencil along an edge at arm's length, keep its tilt and bring it to the paper. Judge it against vertical or horizontal, never against the edge of the drawing board.

    Enlargement calculator

    Scaling multiplies every length by the same factor k; area grows by k². A point measured from the top-left corner of the original lands at k times those distances on the target. The diagram overlays both at the same corner: the shared diagonal is why a proportional enlargement never distorts.
